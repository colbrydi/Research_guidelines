# Dirk's 3x3 Reflection Process

## What Is a 3x3?

Each week I write a "3x3 Reflection" consisting of:

- Three Top Projects This Week
- Three Questions/Challenges
- Three Projects for Next Week

Although the final document is shared with others, I primarily think of it as a reflection rather than a report. The act of writing the 3x3 is often more valuable than the finished product.

---

# Why I Write 3x3 Reflections

The 3x3 serves three primary purposes.

## 1. Weekly Reflection

The most important audience is myself.

Each week, the 3x3 forces me to:

- Review all of my active projects.
- Reflect on what I actually accomplished.
- Consider whether I am spending my time on the right things.
- Identify challenges before they become problems.
- Decide what deserves my attention next week.

In practice, the reflection process is often more valuable than the final document.

The 3x3 helps prevent me from focusing only on the loudest or most urgent project. It forces me to mentally check in across all of my responsibilities and make conscious decisions about priorities.

---

## 2. Team Communication

I share these reflections with students, collaborators, community partners, and colleagues.

The goal is to:

- Keep teams informed about what I am working on.
- Provide context for my priorities.
- Surface challenges where others may be able to help.
- Create opportunities for collaboration.
- Encourage communication across teams.

I encourage (and sometimes require) members of my teams to create similar reflections.

Many useful conversations begin because someone notices a challenge in a 3x3 and reaches out with advice, experience, references, or support.

Because of this, the **Questions/Challenges** section is especially important.

Whenever possible, I try to include questions that:

- Are real.
- Are specific enough for people to respond to.
- Could benefit from outside perspectives.
- Invite discussion instead of simply reporting frustration.

A useful challenge is often worth more than another status update.

---

## 3. Long-Term Record Keeping

The 3x3 also serves as a lightweight professional journal.

Over time, these reflections become a searchable record of:

- Teaching activities
- Student mentoring
- Grant development
- Research collaborations
- Software projects
- Community engagement
- Professional service
- Personal projects

I frequently use past 3x3s when preparing:

- Annual reviews
- Activity reports
- Promotion materials
- Biosketches
- Grant proposals
- Recommendation letters
- Presentations

Because of this, I often include project names, presentation titles, workshop names, and other details that may be useful to remember later.

# Most Important Rule

I strongly prefer that all nine bullets represent different projects whenever possible.

For example:

**Bad**

- CMSE 802 accomplishment
- CMSE 802 challenge
- CMSE 802 plan

**Better**

- CMSE 802
- CMSE 495
- VIP
- Graduate Mentoring
- Community Partners
- Grant Development
- Educational Infrastructure
- Professional Service
- Personal Project

The goal is breadth.

A reader should finish the 3x3 with a reasonable understanding of the variety of things I work on.

---

# Think in Terms of Projects, Not Tasks

When writing a 3x3, focus on the larger project behind the work.

Examples of tasks:

- Sent emails
- Fixed a bug
- Reviewed code
- Graded assignments
- Attended meetings

Examples of projects:

- CMSE 802
- CMSE 495
- VIP / SEE-Insight
- Spring Capstones
- CMSE Course Template
- EASE Reporting
- MailMerge
- Dissertation Mentoring
- Grant Development

The challenge is often identifying which project is represented by a collection of tasks.

# Preferred Structure

## Top Three Projects This Week

This section highlights accomplishments, milestones, discoveries, or meaningful progress.

Projects do not need to be completed.

Good examples:

- Finished a new curriculum module.
- Conducted over-the-shoulder code reviews.
- Delivered an invited presentation.
- Held a productive grant-planning meeting.
- Completed a major software feature.

Avoid generic activity reports.

Examples to avoid:

- Worked on CMSE 802.
- Answered emails.
- Went to meetings.

---

## Questions / Challenges

This is often the most valuable section.

Avoid generic statements such as:

- I am busy.
- I need more time.
- I have too many meetings.
- I am behind on everything.

Instead focus on:

- Design questions
- Curriculum questions
- Research questions
- Software engineering questions
- Organizational challenges
- Leadership questions
- Project tradeoffs
- Emerging opportunities

Good examples:

- Should CMSE develop a Research Software Engineering course?
- How much course infrastructure actually helps students?
- How should AI tools be used safely with protected data?
- How much student behavior can be meaningfully quantified?
- How should accessibility requirements balance flexibility and consistency?
- What support systems help researchers maintain productive habits?

The best challenge items:

- Reflect genuine uncertainty.
- Invite feedback.
- Give readers an opportunity to help.
- Encourage discussion.

A useful challenge often starts with:

> "I have been thinking about..."

or

> "I continue to wonder..."

rather than:

> "I am behind on..."

---

## Projects for Next Week

These should be concrete commitments.

Prefer projects that are not already heavily represented elsewhere in the same 3x3.

Examples:

- Review a dissertation.
- Prepare an invited talk.
- Write recommendation letters.
- Support a workshop.
- Continue capstone outreach.
- Publish a software release.
- Launch a new feature.

These items generally require little explanation.

---

# Writing Style

Keep bullets concise.

Usually:

- One sentence.
- Occasionally two.
- Rarely more than three.

Avoid:

- Corporate language.
- Annual-report language.
- Long explanations.
- Excessive justification.

The project should generally speak for itself.

When editing, ask:

> Can I remove this sentence and still understand the point?

If yes, remove it.

---

# Personal Projects Are Allowed

I occasionally include personal projects.

Examples:

- Family activities
- Cars
- Hobbies
- Travel
- Personal development

This is intentional.

I want students and colleagues to understand that I have interests and responsibilities outside of work.

Personal items should not dominate the report, but including one occasionally helps make the reflection feel authentic and human.

# Things That Make a Great 3x3

A great 3x3:

- Represents nine different projects.
- Includes at least one thoughtful question.
- Includes at least one item that could spark discussion.
- Includes concrete accomplishments.
- Includes realistic commitments for next week.
- Gives readers an opportunity to help.
- Provides useful context without excessive detail.
- Feels personal.
- Is short enough that people will actually read it.

---

# Final Guiding Principle

A good 3x3 is not simply a record of what happened this week.

It is a lightweight tool for:

- Reflection
- Communication
- Planning
- Collaboration
- Record keeping

The best 3x3s help me understand:

- What I accomplished.
- What actually mattered.
- What is challenging.
- What I should prioritize next.
- Where others may be able to provide ideas, experience, feedback, or support.

If there is a choice between completeness and readability, choose readability.

If there is a choice between activity and reflection, choose reflection.
